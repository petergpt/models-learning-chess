# Own exchange review after benchmark 96

Use the actual last board after a capture chain. A recapturing piece remains unless the opponent really captures it later. These are my own observations, without engine analysis.

- Actual 11 Qxd8 Bxd8 12 Bxd8: both queens disappear, then my bishop disappears. White's bishop survives on d8. Rf8xd8 was not played; Rd1xd8 would mate, with Bc8 obstructing Ra8.
- Actual 20 Rxc6 bxc6: White's rook and my bishop disappear. My original b-pawn survives on c6. This gives me a two-point exchange gain, not a fair bishop pair.
- Actual 34 Bxf5+ Kxf5: my rook and White's bishop disappear. My king survives on f5. This costs me two points. The earlier en-passant capture had removed the pawn guard that would prohibit that king recapture.
- Actual 53 Bxh2+ Kxh2: my pawn and White's bishop disappear. My king survives on h2 and my rook remains on g2. This gains two points.
- Actual 84 bxa8=Q: my rook disappears. White's pawn becomes a queen on a8; its original a-pawn still remains on a7. Black has king and c-pawn only.
- Hypothetical enemy rook takes my c6 pawn, my rook takes that enemy rook: my recapturing rook survives. This would win a rook for a pawn if the last recapture is legal and has no further enemy capture. It is not a two-rook liquidation. No such chain is claimed to have occurred in game 96.

After choosing a candidate, mentally place the actual bodies after the opponent's cheapest legal capture and my last legal recapture. Include a further real capture if one exists. A king guard is usable only if the capture destination is safe; naming a nearby king is insufficient.
