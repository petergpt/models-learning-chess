The opening diverged from games 1 and 3 at 8...a6, instead of ...a5. After 9.a5 I kept the bishop on a7. The actual 10...d5 11.exd5 Nxd5 exchanged the central pawns without the pawn loss seen in game 3's later ...d5 sequence. This is a different position and continuation, not a controlled test establishing that ...a6 or this opening plan is better.

The central exchanges remained materially balanced. After 17.Nc5 attacked both Qd7 and Be6, 17...Qc8 18.Nxe6 Qxe6 exchanged a knight for a bishop. Later, 24.Bxd5 Rxd5 25.dxe5 Rxe5 26.Qxe6 Rxe6 exchanged another pair of minor pieces, the central pawns and the queens. After 27.Rfe1 Rxe1+ 28.Bxe1 Rd8, each side had a rook, bishop, knight and five pawns. This actual material count is useful evidence that the recapture sequence worked; it does not establish the best available moves.

The advanced a-pawn remained a concrete constraint. When White played 30.Nd4, I retained the bishop's blockade of a6 and chose ...Rb8, followed by ...c5 and ...Nd5. A contemplated capture on d4 would have vacated a7 and required checking the subsequent pawn advance, not stopping the calculation after a nominal pawn gain. That rejected line was not played and is not a verified success against an opponent's reply.

The clearest successful plan was the exchange of the advanced pawns. The actual sequence was 40...b2 41.Rd1+ Kc6 42.Rb1 Rb8 43.Kd3 Rb6 44.Kc2 Rxa6 45.Rxb2. The pawn on b2 attacked Ra1; White first moved the rook with check, then put it on b1. My rook defended b2 from b8 and then from b6, where it also attacked a6. Capturing a6 allowed White to take b2, leaving four pawns each and freeing the bishop from its blockade. This was the intended exchange and occurred on the board. Do not describe White's entire response as forced: Rd1 also controlled the promotion square b1 along the first rank.

After 45...Rb6 46.Rxb6+ Bxb6, recapturing with the bishop kept my king central. The later 49...g5 removed g7 from the c3–d4–e5–f6–g7 diagonal before the actual 51.Bc3+ check. The continuation 50.e4 f5 51.Bc3+ Ke6 52.exf5+ Kxf5 removed White's central passer in exchange for my f-pawn. Each side then had a bishop and three pawns. These are concrete instances of checking a changed line and the final king recapture, not evidence that all alternative continuations were solved.

After 55.h4, all three pawn pairs were blocked: c4/c5, g3/g4 and h4/h5. The maneuver ...Bc7–d6 put my bishop on a square that both defended c5 and attacked g3. In the final position, White's Be1 defended g3, g3 defended h4, and Kd3 defended c4. My king was on f5. I did not demonstrate a safe breakthrough and chose to repeat. The same position with White to move occurred after 56...Bd6, 58...Kf5 and 60...Kf5; the referee recorded the third occurrence as a draw. This is a measured repetition result, not a proof that every alternative in the bishop ending was drawn.

There is positive local evidence in preserving material through the exchanges, carrying out the a-pawn/b-pawn exchange, and coordinating the bishop on d6. It is limited evidence. Some speculative lines still needed corrections to king adjacency, blocked bishop rays, pawn attacks and the support of a promotion square. Analysis was often much longer than the useful comparison required. Neither that length, the absence of illegal submissions, nor this one draw establishes sustained learning or an Elo improvement. The match record remains 2/5 points.

Keep the existing short cue: rebuild the position after the candidate, inspect the opponent's forcing reply, and verify the final recapture literally. In a passer exchange, include the opponent's promotion reply and identify which rook still guards which pawn. In a bishop ending, trace both diagonals from a candidate square and name the pawn each line protects or attacks. Stop extending an unplayed line once its assumptions are no longer reliable; carry forward the actual evidence rather than an imagined result.
