The opening followed game 5 through 13.Bg5, then I chose 13...Qd7 instead of ...Qd6. That change did not prevent a serious calculation error. After 16...Nf4, White played 17.Bxf4 and took the knight. My intended ...exf4 recapture had overlooked the opened attack on Be6. In the unplayed continuation 17...exf4 18.Bxe6+ Qxe6 19.Qxe6+, there is no black knight able to recapture on e6: the knight on f4 has already been captured, and Nc6 cannot reach e6. I recognized this only after submitting ...Nf4. The actual 17...Bxc4 18.Qxc4+ Qd5 19.Qxd5+ Rxd5 was damage control and left me a minor piece down. The eventual win does not validate ...Nf4 as a sacrifice or justify repeating it.

Active pieces and passed pawns supplied practical counterplay. The actual 20...Rxd3 won a pawn; the subsequent bishop and rook exchanges left White with an extra knight. After 26.Ke2 attacked Rd3, 26...e4 both defended that rook and attacked Nf3. The sequence 27.Nfd2 Nxc3+ 28.Nxc3 Rxc3 exchanged knights and captured c3. White then took e4, but 29...Rc4 attacked both Ne4 and b4; 30.Nc5 Rxb4 won b4. At that point White had a rook, knight and four pawns against my rook and six pawns. These are actual material changes, not a claim that the resulting position was objectively sufficient compensation.

The king route through f7/e6/d5/c5 accompanied the c-pawn's advance. Later, ...Kb5–c4–b3 brought it close enough to support the rook and passer. After 45.Ne5+ Kb3 46.Rc1 c2, Kb3 defended both Rb2 and c2. Then 47...Kc3 attacked Nd3, and 48.Ke2 defended the knight. The blockade was a concrete interaction among the king, rook, knight and pawn; merely calling the passer advanced would miss those dependencies.

The second passer came from 48...b5 49.axb6 Rxb6. White's capture was en passant; my rook recaptured on b6, removing White's last queenside pawn and freeing a6 to advance. This was not an ...axb6 pawn recapture: a pawn on a6 cannot capture a pawn on b6. After 50.Ne1 attacked c2 along with Rc1, 50...Rb2 restored the rook's defense of c2. Kc3 alone would not make ...Kxc2 legal after Rxc2, because Ne1 attacks c2. Keeping the rook and king in place preserved the blockade while another pawn advanced.

The actual 51.g4 fxg4 52.hxg4 left g5 blocking White's g4-pawn. I advanced a6–a5–a4–a3–a2 while White advanced e3–e4–e5–e6–e7. Both sides therefore had immediate promotion concerns. My earlier analysis of this race was unreliable: I repeatedly treated ...c1=Q as though White could simply answer Rxc1. I had missed the rook check uncovered when c2 became empty. I corrected that mistake at move 56, before submitting 56...a1=Q. I did not foresee this complete winning mechanism when starting the a-pawn advance.

The decisive actual sequence was 56...a1=Q 57.Rxa1 c1=Q+. The first promotion drew Rc1 to a1. The second promotion vacated c2, opening Rb2's line through c2 and d2 to Ke2. That check came from the rook. Rxc1 would not answer it, so the newly promoted queen could not simply be captured in reply. This is the clearest successful calculation in this game: the final recapture had to be tested against the changed board and its newly opened check, not counted as an automatic exchange.

After 58.Kf1, I played 58...Qf4+ 59.Kg1 Qg3+, and White resigned. In the final position, my manual inspection gives three legal responses to the check: 60.Kf1 Qf2#, 60.Kh1 Qh2#, or 60.Ng2 Qxg2#. Rb2 supports each mating queen square and cuts off the second rank. These continuations were not played; the measured result is a resignation after 118 plies, not a delivered checkmate. No engine or automated analysis certified these variations.

The evidence is mixed. I converted active king and rook play into two advanced passers, identified the discovered-check promotion tactic before executing it, and maintained the checking sequence to the final mating threat. But I first lost a knight through an unverified recapture, and the promotion race contained a crucial missed check until very late. Other speculative lines still confused pawn capture direction, knight attacks and legal king recaptures. Long analysis often repeated those errors instead of resolving them efficiently. One Black win and a tied score do not establish sustained improvement, an Elo increase, or best play earlier in the game. All results remain in the match record.

Retain the short cue: rebuild the board after a candidate, inspect the opponent's forcing reply, and verify the final recapture literally. This game adds a concrete application: when a pawn leaves a line, inspect the check or attack uncovered behind it before counting captures or promotion tempi. Also verify which pieces still exist after an exchange; a captured knight cannot supply a later recapture. Do not promote the mistaken ...Nf4 line into a successful opening lesson merely because the game was won.
